How to speedrun IELTS?

Short guide for technical people on getting a sufficient IELTS score for university applications while minimizing the time spent studying for it.

I took the exam once and got an overall score of 8, split in 8.5 (listening), 9 (reading), 8 (writing), and 7 (speaking). This is not some crazy result, but my goal (and probably yours) was to waste as little time as possible on this life-changing, heart-breaking, crucial for my future exam. I prepared alone for around 3 weeks in parallel to working full-time. I made up a few simple strategies while studying which can hopefully help you too:

Listening: (8.5/9)

Learn to read a few questions while the narrator is explaining the instructions. While reading the questions I always try to “guess” the word for each gap. After a few tests you’d be surprised how good you’ll get at guessing the words (or their synonyms). By the end of the training season I was able to nail around 2-3 words per text “for free” (which is not bad if you exclude proper nouns). You will also learn the grammatical structures to listen for. You can squeeze some of the time to “check your answers” for reading the next questions.

Reading: (9/9)

I first read the full text at my own pace and try to understand it (usually 5-7 min, up to 9-10 min for the hardest text). While reading I try to visualize details from the sentences in order to feel more entertained and stay focused. After that I go through the questions one by one. The answers for most texts (which are not scrambled) are chronologically ordered. I use that to greatly reduce the area in which I search for the answer. Reading the whole text before starting with the questions may be too time consuming for complex texts so I try to squeeze time from the easier ones. This results in spending around 15,20,25 minutes each text respectively. To avoid getting stuck, I have a strict limit that text 1 must be completed by minute 20, text 2 by minute 40 etc.

Writing: (8/9)

I split my time to be 25 minutes for the short writing and 35 for the long one. (unlike the 20-40 guideline) To evaluate my performance while practicing I tested different models and decided to stick to Gemini pro to evaluate my performance. The main theme when being graded was that spelling errors seem to drag down the scores for multiple sections. For the short writing I always split my text in four paragraphs - introduction, describing data A, describing data B, comparisons+conclusion. By “data A” and “data B” I mean splitting the information in two equally sized parts. For example if you’re given 6 sports to compare on a diagram - you try to split them 3-3, if you’re given a map - A is the old one and B is the new one, etc. To improve my vocabulary score I have a shortlist of “complicated” words which I am comfortable with and I try to utilize them in all texts. In the longer one I always try to steer the topic towards my subfield (if that does not compromise task achievement) where basic terminology is seen as advanced lexical capacity. So, quants talk about “evolution of the market economy in the Southern colonies” and research people talk about their latest project.

Speaking: (7/9)

Learn to make up stories and steer them towards vocabulary that you are comfortable with. For speaking, I practiced by either recording myself talk or using the voice model of chatgpt. These models are bad at evaluating you as most transcribe the audio and then work with the text, but they are useful to practice taking turns in a conversation. The lack of a reproducible practice environment probably impacted my score.

General tips:

Do either full tests or only the sections which you struggle most with. There is no point to optimize reading/listening after constantly achieving 7.5+ as the strictest requirement I have seen is “7 on each section and 7.5 average” which means that 7.5-7.5-7-7 is enough. Book the date early to evade procrastinating and have time for retakes. Learn to write and speak in support of things which you don’t necessarily agree with if you find it easier to build an argument around them.
