The manufacturing of bricks for the building industry involves seven steps as shown on the diagram. Each step can roughly be categorised as part of either extraction, processing or logistics.

Firstly, clay has to be dug up from the ground by diggers. Clay lumps need to be further split apart into finer pieces. This is done through a metal grid and a moving roller which break apart big lumps.
In order to form actual bricks, sand and water must be added to the clay and the mixture must be passed through a wire cutter or a mould to get the correct shape. 

The resulting bricks are laid out in a drying oven where they spend between 24 and 48 hours to dry up. After that they undergo three thermal processes. The first one exposes the bricks to moderate heating at 200 to 980 degrees Celsius. The second step includes high heat processing at 870 to 1300 degrees. 
The thermal processing is completed after the bricks stay in a cooling chamber from 48 to 72 hours.

The bricks' manufacturing process is completed after they are packaged and delivered to their final destination.




Innovation in commercial transport and specifically aviation has drastically increased the number of tourists in many places. This process has brought considerable monetary influx in tourist hubs but also concerns about the well-being of local inhabitants and the environment. 

Most places which profit from international tourism become busy in a single season of the year which leads to a surge in demand for housing, public transport and entertainment. I believe that the inability to match the demand with the available supply leads to problems for local inhabitants which outweigh the benefits of tourism. For example, homeowners who prefer to rent out their apartments to tourists cause difficulties to locals who are searching for permanent housing. Furthermore, the transport infrastructure has to simultaneously handle tourists in addition to locals commuting to work which leads to delays and increased traffic.

Problems in traffic also contribute to the environmental damage caused by tourism. A surge in the number of vehicles and people increases the temperatures in the city and worsens the air quality which directly affects wildlife. Moreover, littering and incorrect trash disposal harm animal and plant species which inhabit the city and its outskirts. Although cities try to take measures against littering such as better trash collection systems and more signs forbidding littering, it is a common sight to see trash on streets and overfilled bins in popular destinations.

In conclusion, I think that the financial burdens caused to locals and the detrimental effects on the environment are concerns which outweigh the monetary benefits and the cultural diversity which tourism brings.
