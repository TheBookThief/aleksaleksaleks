The graph shows the number of closures and openings of shops in a given country for the period between 2011 and 2018.
The number of shops that closed in the first four years of the period fluctuated by at most 1500 on a yearly basis with closures in 2011 and 2014 being around 6500, roughly 6000 in 2012 and a bit more than 7000 in 2013. 2015 was marked by a sharp decline in closures with fewer than a 1000. Since 2016 the closures approached their previous quantities but stayed almost stationary at a bit more than 5000 per year.
Openings in the period were marked by an overall downwards trend with the maximum number of newly opened shops being in 2011 with approximately 8500 newly opened venues. In 2012 this quantity decreased by more than half to fewer than 4000. The following 2 years were marked by almost linear growth with 2013 having 5000 new openings and 2014 a bit more than 6000. Another steep decline was observed in 2015 when openings returned to their 2012 levels at around 4000 new shops for the year. Little oscillation was seen between 2015 and 2017 when the number of openings remained almost the same. 2018 marked the year with the lowest count of newly opened shops at roughly 3000.
Overall, the closures consistently outnumbered openings representing the majority of the period in 6 out of the 8 years present on the graph. However, the two quantities stayed close throughout the whole period except for 2015.


The amount of information available around us has skyrocketed since the creation of the Internet. This has irreversibly led to people being exposed to enormous amounts of diverse and not always correct information on sensitive topics including medicine and health.
The desire to care for one's health and that of others is natural, but the consequences of inadequate and unprofessional judgement are far too great. Misinterpreting symptoms or delaying visits to one's doctor because of alternative treatments can expose patients to unnecessary risk when fighting time sensitive diseases. Examples of illnesses where medical intervention by a doctor is time critical include many types of cancer, leukemia, and tuberculosis. Furthermore, if no evidence is backing up the unusual treatments, there is not only a risk of being scammed, but also the danger of worsening an individual's health, contrary to what one is doing. 
Clinically approved methods do not always work, and in those cases, it is natural to search for alternatives to save oneself, which may lead to trying out experimental medicines. However, I still do not think the health risks involved are worth it. Even in cases of terminal diseases, the chances of a scientifically unproven treatment to magically save one's life are slim to none. In those moments, it is crucial for one to visit even more doctors and explore further everything that is known in the scientific world about their condition.
Ultimately, despite the abundance of alternative medical solutions available, I believe the responsible approach to one's health problems remains trusting qualified professionals for diagnoses, medicine prescriptions, and treatments.
